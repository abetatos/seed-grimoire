# Relationship craft — the space between people

Used by `plan-book` (the `## Relaciones` section of `plan/arcs.md`) and by
`critique-plan` as the bar for relational depth. Companion to
`references/character-craft.md` (which designs the people) — this designs
the **space between them**.

Depth in fiction lives **between** characters more than inside them. A
character arc is one person changing. A **relationship arc** is the space
between two people changing — and it is a separate designed object, not a
by-product of two individual arcs colliding. Most books that "feel thin
despite good characters" have designed the people and left the web between
them to chance. The web is where the reader's heart actually lives.

---

## 1. The dyad as an object

For each important pair, design the relationship the way you design a
character — as its own thing with a beginning, a turn, and an end. A dyad
has five load-bearing fields:

- **What A wants FROM B** — the specific thing (to be seen, to be
  forgiven, to be used, to be free of them). Not "they're friends."
- **What B wants FROM A** — usually **not** the same thing (see §2). The
  gap between the two wants is the relationship's engine.
- **The misread** — what each gets *wrong* about the other. Bruno reads
  Mauro's attention as being seen; Mauro's attention is study. The reader
  can hold the true version while the characters hold the wrong one — that
  gap is dramatic irony you designed on purpose.
- **The turn** — the one scene where the dynamic **flips**: the want is
  granted and curdles, the misread is corrected, the debt comes due. Pin
  it to a chapter. A relationship with no turn is a static bond, not an
  arc.
- **The end-state** — what the space between them is at the close, and what
  it cost to get there.

Write these as a compact block per pair. They are diagnostic for plan and
critique only; **prose never names them** (same rule as the sliding
scales).

---

## 2. Asymmetry — the strongest dyads are lopsided

Mutual, symmetric bonds (two people who love each other the same way, want
the same thing from each other) are flat — there's no gap to move. The
dyads that carry a book are **asymmetric**:

- A loves B as a father; B needs A as a **subject of study**.
- A wants B's forgiveness; B doesn't know A did anything.
- A would die for B; B has already decided to spend A.

The asymmetry is the tension, and **closing or inverting** it is the arc.
When you find yourself writing "they care about each other," stop and ask:
*care as what, and does the other return it in the same currency?* If yes,
make one side want something the other can't give.

---

## 3. Triangulation — no dyad in a vacuum

A pair alone is stable. A **third person** is what applies pressure and
forces the turn. Every load-bearing dyad should be squeezed by at least one
outside relationship:

- The hunter who makes the tutor choose between studying the boy and
  shielding him.
- The rival whose arrival forces two allies to rank their loyalties.

When a relationship stalls in the plan, the fix is usually **not** a
confrontation between the two — it's introducing a third whose want makes
the dyad's buried tension unavoidable.

---

## 4. Foils and mirrors — relationships that argue the theme

Some pairs exist to carry the theme the plot can't state:

- **Foil dyad** — two people in the *same* situation who make **opposite
  choices**. The protagonist↔antagonist pair is the master foil (same
  wound, opposite cure — see `character-craft.md` §4), but a book wants
  more than one: a secondary foil in the ensemble sharpens the argument
  from a second angle.
- **Mirror dyad** — a relationship that shows the protagonist what they
  could have / could still become. The reader measures the protagonist
  against it.

**Name which pairs are foils and what thesis they argue.** A foil that
argues the *same* thing as the main pair is redundant; each should attack
the theme from a different side (identity vs. loyalty vs. faith).

---

## 5. The relationship as seed carrier

The deepest "woven" feeling (`references/seed-craft.md`, emotional spine)
often comes from a **relationship's transformation landing on a planted
image**. The gesture planted early — a hand held to a face, a shared word,
an object passed — whose *meaning inverts* at the turn. Design at least one
dyad whose turn pays off a `Resolution image` seed: the same image, the
opposite feeling. That is what makes a relationship's end *ache* instead of
merely conclude.

---

## 6. The floor — how many designed dyads

A book needs, at minimum:

- **protagonist ↔ antagonist** — always, designed as the master foil.
- **protagonist ↔ deuteragonist / closest bond** — the emotional spine.
- **at least one dyad that does NOT involve the protagonist** — two
  secondaries (or a secondary and the antagonist) with their own thing
  between them. This is the single strongest cure for "the world revolves
  only around the hero"; it's what makes the cast feel like a society
  rather than a hub-and-spokes.

For an epic/trilogy, scale up: ≥ 3–4 designed dyad arcs in Book I, at least
one of them carrying into the later books (a relationship whose turn is
deferred is a trans-book thread — see the attrition ledger in
`critique-grimoire`).

---

## Quick checklist (per book)

- [ ] Every important pair has a **dyad block**: A-wants-from-B,
      B-wants-from-A, the misread, the turn (pinned to a chapter),
      the end-state.
- [ ] At least the master foil (protag↔antag) is **asymmetric** and its
      turn is planted.
- [ ] Every load-bearing dyad is **triangulated** by a third want.
- [ ] **Foil pairs named**, each arguing the theme from a different side.
- [ ] ≥ 1 dyad that **doesn't involve the protagonist**.
- [ ] ≥ 1 dyad whose turn lands on a **`Resolution image` seed**.
