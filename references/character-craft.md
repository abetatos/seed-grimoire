# Character craft — making people, not roles

Used by `book-setup` and `plan-book` when designing the cast, and by
`critique-grimoire` / `critique-plan` as the bar for what "good" looks
like. The Wound / Want / Need / Lie skeleton (`references/fantasy-beats.md`,
Character arcs) is the **floor**, not the ceiling: it produces coherent
characters, not *interesting* ones. A character can have all four bullets
filled and still be flat. This doc is what makes them worth reading.

Four levers turn a role into a person: a **contradiction**, a **want on
the page**, a **magic↔lie interlock**, and — for the villain — a **dark
mirror**. Then the cast needs **an ensemble** around the principals and a
**divergence pass** so no one is the first idea that came to mind.

---

## 1. The contradiction — the engine of surprise

Every memorable character holds one **live contradiction**: two true things
about them that should not both be true. The gentle man who is an
executioner. The coward who is fearless about exactly one thing. The
believer who keeps breaking the rule they'd die for. The contradiction is
not a flaw and not hypocrisy — it is a person too large for a single
adjective.

- **Name it in one line.** "Mauro: the tenderness of the gesture and the
  coldness of the purpose live in the same hand." If you cannot name the
  contradiction, the character is a function.
- A character with **no** contradiction is coherent and dead — the reader
  predicts them after one scene. The contradiction is what makes the next
  scene un-guessable while staying in character.
- The **arc** is usually the contradiction resolving, collapsing, or
  finally being paid for. Don't resolve it early; it's the tension that
  carries them.
- **Test:** can the character surprise the reader *without acting out of
  character*? If every surprise requires them to behave unlike themselves,
  they have no contradiction — only a mask that drops.

Require a contradiction for **every principal** (protagonist, antagonist,
deuteragonist). SHOULD-give one to any secondary who carries a scene.

---

## 2. The want on the page — everyone wants something now

Vonnegut's rule: every character who appears should want something, even
if it's only a glass of water. Two wants operate at once:

- **The plot-want** — the arc-level Want (survive, belong, avenge). Slow.
- **The scene-want** — what they are trying to get *in this room, right
  now*, from the person across from them. Fast. Concrete. Usually
  unspoken.

Flat scenes are scenes where only the protagonist wants something and
everyone else is furniture delivering information. The fix is not more
description — it's giving the other body in the room a want that
**crosses** the protagonist's. A shopkeeper who wants the boy gone before
the tax-reader comes. A tutor who wants the lesson to end so he can study
the child. Two wants pulling against each other IS a scene.

- **Design-time obligation:** for every named character who *speaks* in a
  chapter, the plan should be able to answer "what do they want in that
  scene?" — even if the answer is trivial and never stated.
- **Prose-time tell:** the reader never needs the want named. They feel it
  in what the character steers toward and away from.

---

## 3. The magic↔lie interlock — psychology made mechanical

In a hard-magic book the system is not set dressing bolted onto the
characters — it is the most powerful tool you have for **externalizing the
interior**. The best hard-magic protagonists have a relationship to the
magic that *is* their wound and their Lie in mechanical form.

- El-vitral's model: Bruno believes *"I'm worth what I can do"* (the Lie);
  his magic is a sink that **preys upward on the coloured** and erodes the
  self — so *to be seen is to be endangered*, and using his worth destroys
  what he loves. The system is the psychology. The reader watches the
  theme operate without a word of exposition.
- **Require the interlock for EVERY principal, not just the protagonist.**
  A deuteragonist whose relationship to the magic is generic ("she's a
  skilled reader") wastes the book's strongest instrument. Ask: how does
  *this* character's specific relation to the system dramatize *their*
  Lie? The scholar who can read every colour but her own. The zealot whose
  power depends on the belief the plot will break.
- The **cost** each character pays should hurt them where their wound
  already is — not a generic exhaustion. Cost is characterization.

---

## 4. The antagonist as dark mirror — beyond "has a thesis"

"The antagonist must have a thesis, not be evil" is the floor
(`critique-grimoire` makes a missing thesis a MUST-fix). The ceiling is
the **dark mirror**:

- The strongest antagonist **shares the protagonist's wound** and chose the
  **opposite solution**. Same injury, divergent road. The antagonist is the
  person the protagonist could have become — which is why the confrontation
  is about the theme, not about survival.
- The persuasive antagonist is **right about the diagnosis, wrong about the
  cure.** They have seen the same rot the protagonist has; the reader
  should catch themselves nodding before recoiling. If the reader never
  agrees for even a paragraph, the thesis is decoration.
- Separate the **proximate** antagonist (the hunter, the inquisitor — the
  face across the table) from the **systemic** one (the institution, the
  law of the magic, the world's arrangement). A book with only a proximate
  villain feels small; only a systemic one feels bloodless. Name both, and
  make the proximate one a *product* of the systemic one — a true believer,
  not a hypocrite.
- **The mirror is a relationship** (see `references/relationship-craft.md`
  §4): design the protagonist↔antagonist foil as a dyad, not two separate
  arcs that happen to collide.

---

## 5. The ensemble — a cast, not two leads and a crowd

"Faltan personajes" is almost never a shortage of *principals* — it's a
world with no **memorable secondaries**. The principals + antagonist carry
the plot; the ensemble is what makes the world feel **populated and
lived-in**. Design it at plan-time, not reactively (the critique's
"named secondary functioning as a prop" need-test is a *detector*; by then
it's too late — the person should have been designed before they walked
on).

A book wants **3–6 named secondaries** who are not principals but not
props. Each gets:

- **One physical tell** — a single concrete image that re-identifies them
  on sight (not a full description). The miller with flour permanently in
  the creases of his knuckles.
- **One micro-want of their own** — a want that **predates the protagonist
  and outlasts them**. It is *their* story, glimpsed. The widow who wants
  her son's name back on the parish roll. This want does not serve the
  plot; it exists whether or not the hero shows up.
- **One relationship to a principal** — how they read, use, help, or
  obstruct someone who matters.

**The cut-the-protagonist test:** remove the protagonist from the scene. Does
the secondary still want something, still have somewhere to be? If yes, they
are a person. If they go inert — standing there waiting to be talked to — they
are a prop with a name. Fix by giving them their own errand.

- Secondaries are also your best **seed carriers** — a face the reader likes
  can hold a plant for twenty chapters without the reader suspecting the
  plant, because they're watching the person, not the clue.
- Don't over-cast. Six people with a want each beat twenty names. A named
  character with no want and no tell should be **demoted to a role** ("the
  ferryman") or cut.

---

## 6. The divergence pass — kill the first idea

The first version of any character is the genre's average of that role,
because that's what comes to mind first. The apprentice is earnest, the
mentor is wise, the zealot is cruel. Interesting characters come from
**refusing the first idea on purpose.**

Before committing a principal — or a stock role the book leans on — generate
**three genuinely different takes**, not three flavors of one:

- They must diverge on a **load-bearing axis** (the character's flaw, the
  antagonist's thesis, what the mentor actually wants), not on cosmetics
  (hair, name, home region).
- Then **pick one, or graft** the sharpest element of a runner-up onto the
  winner. The goal is a character no reader predicts from the logline.

Apply divergence specifically to: the protagonist's **defining flaw**, the
antagonist's **thesis**, and any **stock role** the plot requires (the
mentor, the love interest, the loyal friend, the rival). Those four are
where cliché hides. `book-setup` runs this as an `AskUserQuestion` — the
author sees three takes and chooses.

---

## Quick checklist (per book)

- [ ] Every principal has a **named contradiction**.
- [ ] Every principal has a **magic↔lie interlock** specific to their Lie.
- [ ] The antagonist is a **dark mirror** (shared wound, opposite cure) and
      is right about the diagnosis; proximate *and* systemic both named.
- [ ] **3–6 ensemble secondaries**, each with a tell + a micro-want that
      survives the cut-the-protagonist test.
- [ ] Every principal (and any stock role) survived a **divergence pass**.
- [ ] Relationships designed as dyads — see
      `references/relationship-craft.md`.
