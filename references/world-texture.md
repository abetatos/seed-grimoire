# World texture — the lived-in world, designed at plan-time

Used by `book-setup` and `plan-book` (promoted into `canon/world.md`) and
by `critique-plan` / `critique-grimoire` as the bar for a world that feels
**inhabited** rather than mapped. Companion to
`references/dwelling-techniques.md`: that is the **prose-time** technique
for lingering; this is the **design-time catalog** the prose spends. Design
the texture before writing so it is *load-bearing material*, not filler
improvised to hit a mood.

Geography + factions + history are a world's **skeleton**. They tell you
where things are and who rules. But a reader *believes* a world through its
**ordinary life** — how people eat, earn, curse, and mark the days — none
of which a map shows. A book with a rich magic system and five named cities
can still feel like a stage set if no one ever seems to *live* there.

---

## 1. The economy of the magic — who profits, who pays

The most under-designed thing in fantasy worlds. If magic exists, ordinary
(non-heroic) life is organized **around** it. Answer, concretely:

- **What work exists because of the system?** Jobs, guilds, trades that
  only make sense in this world (colour-readers, sink-wardens, prism-grinders).
- **Who profits and who is taxed?** The magic stratifies the world
  economically (`critique-grimoire` 3b) — make that visible in prices,
  levies, tolls, wages. What does a loaf cost a plebe vs. a noble?
- **What is the black market / the workaround?** Where there's a scarce
  power there's smuggling, forgery, a grey trade in it. This is where plot
  and world meet.
- **What does the system make *cheap* that our world makes dear, and vice
  versa?** That inversion is where a world feels genuinely other.

---

## 2. The daily texture — concrete nouns

The reader is grounded by specifics, not by abstractions. Design a small
stock of concrete, recurring nouns:

- **Food and drink** — what the poor eat, what the rich eat, the one dish a
  region is known for. (Bruno understands the world through his craft — a
  POV's vocabulary, `book-setup` 14b, is a texture lever too.)
- **Labor and the working day** — how the day is structured, when the
  bells/whistles/calls sound, what a working week looks like.
- **Money, weights, measures** — name the coin, the unit. "Two coppers"
  grounds harder than "a small sum."
- **Craft and material** — the wood, the pitch, the dye, the stone the
  place lives off. Trades leave marks on bodies (a tell — see
  `character-craft.md` §5).

Keep it a **stock of a dozen or so** concrete items, not an encyclopedia.
The prose spends them; the plan holds them.

---

## 3. The culture — what people say and believe

Voice and worldview, not architecture:

- **Oaths, curses, blessings** — what a character says when they stub a
  toe, greet a stranger, or fear death. One good in-world curse does more
  worldbuilding than a page of history.
- **Slang and idiom** — how a caste or trade talks. The in-world figure of
  speech ("the things are given with both hands").
- **Songs, sayings, superstitions** — the folk belief, the thing children
  chant, the omen everyone half-believes. Superstition is where the
  *common* understanding of the magic (the surface layer,
  `grimoire.md` §2) lives — and where the reader's misreading gets seeded.
- **Courtesy and taboo** — what is polite, what is unspeakable, who bows to
  whom. Manners encode the power structure without exposition.
- **The calendar** — festivals, holy days, market days, mourning rites. A
  named festival is a stage *and* a clock.

---

## 4. Class markers — reading rank at a glance

In a stratified (esp. magic-stratified) world, a reader should be able to
place a stranger's caste from a detail. Design the **at-a-glance markers**:

- Dress, colour, ornament (in el-vitral, colour *is* caste — literalized).
- Speech register and vocabulary.
- Food, posture, where they're allowed to stand.

These do double duty: they characterize *and* they keep the magic's
stratification present on every page without a lecture.

---

## 5. The load-bearing rule — texture must be used

Texture is decoration unless it **works**. Every designed texture element
should do at least one of:

- **gate a plot beat** (the festival that empties the square for the
  chase; the market day that puts the wrong people in one room),
- **mark a character** (the trade that scars the hands; the curse only one
  caste says),
- **carry a seed** (the superstition that turns out to be the buried
  truth; the folk song whose last verse is the payoff).

Design ~a dozen texture elements and, for each, note which of the three
jobs it does. A texture element with no job is a SHOULD-cut — the same
discipline `critique-plan` applies to decorative factions and unused places.
Distribute them so **act 1 is dense** with lived-in texture (the reader is
being *placed* in the world — `fantasy-beats.md`, Inhabitation).

---

## Quick checklist (per book)

- [ ] **Economy of the magic** named: jobs it creates, who profits/pays,
      the black market.
- [ ] A stock of **concrete daily nouns** (food, coin, craft, the working
      day).
- [ ] **Culture**: at least one oath/curse, one piece of slang, one
      superstition, one festival/calendar marker.
- [ ] **Class markers** that let the reader place a stranger at a glance.
- [ ] Every texture element has a **job** (gate a beat / mark a character /
      carry a seed); the jobless ones are cut.
- [ ] Texture weighted toward **act 1** (inhabitation).
